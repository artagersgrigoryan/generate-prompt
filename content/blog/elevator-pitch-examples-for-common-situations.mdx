An elevator pitch that works at a networking mixer will fall flat at an
investor meeting, and one built for a job interview won't land at an industry
conference. The words matter less than most advice suggests — the structure
built for the specific audience and setting is what actually determines whether
it lands.

Here are seven examples across common situations, with notes on why each one is
built the way it is.

## 1. Job interview (mid-career professional)

> "I'm a product manager who specializes in taking B2B tools from early
> traction to their first real scale — usually the 10 to 50 employee stage,
> where the roadmap needs the most discipline. At my last company, I took a
> product from $2M to $9M ARR by focusing the team on three features instead
> of the fifteen we were tracking. I'm looking for a similar inflection point
> where that focus is the missing piece."

**What it's doing:** Names a specific specialty (a stage of company growth) 
instead of a generic title. The result is quantified. The final sentence
connects directly to what the interviewer is likely trying to solve.

## 2. Networking event (casual, first meeting)

> "I help small e-commerce brands fix the parts of their site that lose sales —
> mostly checkout and product pages. Most of my clients come to me after
> they've already tried a redesign that didn't move the needle."

**What it's doing:** Short — under 15 seconds. Ends on a detail ("already tried
a redesign") that invites a natural follow-up question rather than closing the
topic.

## 3. Investor pitch (60-second version)

> "We help mid-market logistics companies cut fuel costs without changing
> their fleet — through route optimization software that plugs into existing
> dispatch systems. Our pilot customer cut fuel spend by 18% in the first
> quarter with zero hardware changes. We're now working with four more
> companies in the same vertical, and the reason this works is that logistics
> companies have been forced to adopt dispatch software over the last five
> years, which means the data we need already exists — we just have to plug
> into it."

**What it's doing:** Leads with outcome and mechanism. Adds the "why now"
context that a 30-second version wouldn't have room for — this is the piece
that makes 60 seconds worth the extra time.

## 4. Career changer (pivoting industries)

> "I spent six years as a high school teacher before moving into corporate
> training last year. What I learned running a classroom of thirty translates
> directly to building training programs that people actually retain — I know
> how to structure information so it sticks, not just how to present it."

**What it's doing:** Addresses the pivot directly instead of hiding it. Frames
the previous career as a source of transferable skill rather than something to
explain away.

## 5. Freelancer or consultant (client-facing)

> "I write technical documentation for developer tools — the kind that
> actually gets used instead of ignored. Most of my clients come to me because
> their existing docs have a high support-ticket rate, and after I rewrite
> them, that number usually drops by 30-40% within two months."

**What it's doing:** Names the specific niche (developer tools) rather than
"technical writer" broadly. The metric is tied to a business outcome (support
tickets), not just "clearer writing."

## 6. Founder introducing their startup casually

> "We built a tool that turns messy expense receipts into categorized
> accounting entries automatically — so small business owners stop spending
> their Sunday nights doing manual data entry. We've got about 400 businesses
> using it right now."

**What it's doing:** Describes the problem in a way anyone can picture (Sunday
night data entry), not just the feature. The traction number is concrete and
believable, not inflated.

## 7. Recent graduate (limited experience)

> "I just finished a degree in data analytics, and during my last internship I
> built a dashboard that the team still uses to track weekly conversion
> rates — it replaced a manual spreadsheet process that took someone two hours
> every Monday. I'm looking for a first analytics role where I can keep
> building things people actually use."

**What it's doing:** Compensates for limited experience with one specific,
verifiable accomplishment rather than a list of coursework. Doesn't apologize
for being early-career — it just shows evidence.

## The pattern across all seven

Most of the pitches above share three things: a specific target (not
"businesses" or "people"), an outcome instead of a process, and exactly one
differentiator instead of several. The career-changer and recent-graduate
versions trade the specific-audience piece for a specific, verifiable
accomplishment instead — because early in a pivot or a career, proof of
capability does more work than an audience statement would. The variation is
in length, formality, and which detail gets emphasized — never in the
underlying discipline of picking one thing to prove and proving it concretely.

If your current pitch doesn't fit into one of the shapes above, that's usually
the reason it isn't landing. Not the wording — the missing specificity underneath it.
