Most LinkedIn summaries read like a resume paragraph — a list of job titles and
responsibilities in third person, front-loaded with an adjective like "results-
driven" or "passionate." They're not wrong, exactly. They're just invisible.
Nobody messages someone because their summary confirmed a job title they
already had listed above it.

Here are five summaries across different roles that are built to generate
messages, not just describe a career, with notes on why each one works.

## Example 1: Marketing professional

> I run growth marketing for B2B SaaS companies between $1M and $10M ARR — the
> stage where the founder-led marketing that got you here stops scaling.
>
> Most recently, I took a company from 200 to 1,400 monthly signups in eight
> months by rebuilding their content funnel around bottom-of-funnel comparison
> pages instead of top-of-funnel blog posts.
>
> If your growth has plateaued and you're not sure whether the problem is
> channel, message, or funnel — that diagnostic is where I'd start.
>
> Open to fractional and consulting engagements.

**What it's doing:** Names a specific stage of company (the $1M–$10M range)
instead of a broad title. States a real result with numbers. Ends with an
invitation that tells the reader exactly what message to send.

## Example 2: Software engineer (job-seeking)

> I build backend systems that handle scale problems most teams haven't hit
> yet — the kind of infrastructure work that gets skipped until it becomes an
> emergency.
>
> At my last company, I rebuilt a payments pipeline that was failing under
> load during peak traffic, cutting error rates from 4% to under 0.1% while
> the system was still live in production.
>
> Currently looking for senior or staff backend roles at companies scaling
> past their first infrastructure ceiling.

**What it's doing:** Explains the type of problem solved, not just the tech
stack. The result is specific and hard to fake. The closing line tells
recruiters exactly what to search for and what to message about.

## Example 3: Career changer into UX design

> I spent seven years as a physical therapist before moving into UX design —
> which sounds unrelated until you realize both jobs are about diagnosing why
> something isn't working for the person in front of you.
>
> I'm now designing patient-facing healthcare software, which lets me use both
> backgrounds at once: I understand clinical workflows most designers don't,
> and I bring a design process most clinicians haven't been exposed to.
>
> Looking for UX roles in health tech or other regulated industries where
> domain knowledge is as valuable as design skill.

**What it's doing:** Frames the career change as an asset instead of
apologizing for it. Names the specific intersection (healthcare + design) that
makes the pivot valuable rather than confusing. Tells the reader exactly which
roles to think of them for.

## Example 4: Consultant or fractional executive

> I've been the first VP of Sales at three Series A startups — which means
> I've built the same thing three times: a sales process, a hiring plan, and a
> forecast that a board actually trusts, all before there was a playbook to
> follow.
>
> I now work with founders at that exact stage on a fractional basis, usually
> for 3-6 months, until there's a full-time hire ready to take over what I
> built.
>
> If you're pre-Series B and sales is still founder-led or chaotic, that's
> the problem I solve.

**What it's doing:** Uses repetition ("built the same thing three times") to
establish pattern-recognition credibility instead of just listing companies.
Defines the engagement model up front (fractional, 3-6 months) so the reader
knows exactly what kind of message to send.

## Example 5: Recent graduate entering the workforce

> I'm a recent data science graduate who spent my last internship building a
> churn prediction model that the team is still using — it replaced a manual
> monthly review process and now flags at-risk accounts automatically each
> week.
>
> I'm looking for my first full-time analytics or data science role, ideally
> somewhere I can keep working close to the business side of the data, not
> just the modeling.
>
> Happy to walk through the churn model project with anyone hiring for
> analytics roles — details available on request.

**What it's doing:** Leads with the one concrete project instead of GPA or
coursework. The closing line lowers the barrier to a first message — "happy to
walk through the project" is a specific, low-effort ask a recruiter can act on.

## What all five have in common

Every summary above:

- States a specific niche or specialty, not a broad job title
- Includes one real, quantified result
- Ends with a clear signal of what kind of message to send and about what
- Is written in first person, in a voice that sounds like an actual person

None of them read as a list of past responsibilities. They read as an answer to
"why should I message this person specifically" — which is the actual question
a recruiter or potential client is asking when they land on a profile.
