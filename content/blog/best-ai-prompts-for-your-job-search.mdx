A full job search generates more writing than most people plan for. Before an
offer, you'll likely write a resume, a cover letter, a LinkedIn summary, an
elevator pitch for networking events, a handful of cold outreach emails, and at
least one thank you note. After you accept, there's often a resignation letter
to write too.

AI can draft a strong first pass on all of it — but only if the prompt you give
it is specific enough to produce something usable. A vague prompt like "write me
a cover letter" produces a generic cover letter. Here's what to actually ask for
at each stage, and what tends to go wrong when people skip the details.

## Stage 1: Resume bullet points

**What to prompt for:** Not "make my resume better" — instead, give the AI your
raw responsibilities and ask it to convert them into achievement-focused
bullets with metrics. Include the actual numbers you have, even rough ones.
"Reduced onboarding time from three weeks to five days" beats "improved
onboarding process" because ATS systems and human recruiters both respond to
specificity.

**What goes wrong:** People ask AI to "make this sound impressive" without
giving it real information to work with, so it invents vague achievement
language instead of grounding bullets in what actually happened.

## Stage 2: The cover letter

**What to prompt for:** Feed the AI the actual job description, not just the
job title. Ask it to identify the top three requirements and mirror that
language back using your specific experience — not generic enthusiasm. A
letter built from the real posting reads as tailored; one written from a job
title alone reads as a template with the company name swapped in.

**What goes wrong:** Cover letters that could apply to any candidate at any
company in the same industry. The tell is a letter with no sentence that
references anything specific from the actual posting.

→ [Cover Letter Generator](/en/tools/cover-letter-generator)

## Stage 3: LinkedIn summary

**What to prompt for:** LinkedIn's About section does two jobs at once — reads
naturally to a human and gets indexed by LinkedIn's search for recruiter
queries. Ask AI to write it with your target role's likely search terms
included naturally, not stuffed in. Give it your actual specialty, not a broad
category — "supply chain analytics for mid-market retailers" beats "operations
professional."

**What goes wrong:** Summaries that read like a resume paragraph instead of a
person — third-person job titles strung together with no voice.

→ [LinkedIn Summary Generator](/en/tools/linkedin-summary-generator)

## Stage 4: The elevator pitch

**What to prompt for:** An elevator pitch prompt needs a target length (30 or
60 seconds), your specific audience (recruiter, networking contact, panel), and
one concrete result you're proud of. Ask for two versions — one short, one
longer — since the right length depends entirely on the setting.

**What goes wrong:** Pitches that list five things you're good at instead of
one thing that's memorable. More detail doesn't make a pitch stronger; it makes
it forgettable.

→ [Elevator Pitch Generator](/en/tools/elevator-pitch-generator)

## Stage 5: Cold outreach to hiring managers or contacts

**What to prompt for:** Give the AI something specific about the person or
company you're reaching out to — a recent post, a company announcement, a
mutual connection — and ask for a short email with one small, clear ask. The
prompt should specify that the ask needs to take less than 20 minutes of the
recipient's time.

**What goes wrong:** Outreach emails that ask for too much too soon ("Can we
grab coffee and discuss opportunities?") instead of one specific, low-friction
next step.

→ [Cold Outreach Email Generator](/en/tools/cold-outreach-email-generator)

## Stage 6: The post-interview thank you email

**What to prompt for:** Ask AI to reference one specific moment from the actual
interview conversation — something discussed, a question you didn't answer as
well as you wanted to, or a detail about the role that came up. Specify that it
should be sent within 24 hours and be short enough to read in under a minute.

**What goes wrong:** Generic thank you notes that could have been sent without
attending the interview at all — no reference to anything that was actually
discussed.

→ [Thank You Email Generator](/en/tools/thank-you-email-generator)

## Stage 7: The resignation letter

**What to prompt for:** Once you've accepted an offer, give the AI your actual
tenure, your relationship with your manager, and how much notice you're giving.
The tone should change based on those inputs — a 10-year employee retiring
writes a different letter than someone leaving after eight months on bad terms.

**What goes wrong:** One-size-fits-all resignation templates that either
overshare (explaining exactly why you're leaving) or undershare (missing the
specific last day, which creates ambiguity later).

## The pattern across all seven

Every prompt that produces something usable shares the same shape: specific
inputs (real numbers, actual job descriptions, concrete situations) plus a
clear constraint (length, tone, audience). The prompts that produce generic
output are missing one or both.
