Most resignation letter advice covers the generic template. That's useful right
up until your situation isn't generic — you're leaving on bad terms, or moving to
a competitor, or retiring after twenty years, and the neutral template suddenly
feels wrong for what you actually need to say.

Here are six examples for situations that come up often, with notes on what each
one is doing differently.

## Example 1: Standard two weeks' notice

> Dear Priya,
>
> I am resigning from my position as Marketing Coordinator at Fenwick Group,
> effective Friday, August 7.
>
> I've appreciated the opportunity to work on the product launch team this past
> year, and I'm committed to a smooth handover. I'm happy to document my current
> projects and help train whoever takes over my responsibilities.
>
> Thank you for the support during my time here.
>
> Sincerely,
> Marcus Webb

**What it's doing:** No explanation, no drama, no extra paragraphs. This is the
letter for the most common case — an amicable departure with a standard notice
period. It works precisely because it doesn't try to do more than notify and
offer a transition.

## Example 2: Leaving for a competitor

> Dear David,
>
> Please accept this letter as formal notice of my resignation from my role as
> Account Executive, effective two weeks from today, June 12.
>
> I want to be direct: I've accepted a position elsewhere in the industry. I
> understand this may raise questions about client handoffs and non-compete
> terms, and I'm glad to work through those with you and legal before my last day.
>
> I'm grateful for what I learned here and will do what I can to leave things in
> good order.
>
> Regards,
> Elena Chen

**What it's doing:** Names the sensitivity directly instead of pretending it
isn't there. Addressing the competitor question head-on, briefly and without
detail, closes off the awkward follow-up conversation before it starts. Notice
it still doesn't name the company — that's not required and adds nothing.

## Example 3: Leaving on bad terms

> Dear Ms. Alvarez,
>
> I am submitting my resignation from my position as Operations Analyst,
> effective July 30, two weeks from the date of this letter.
>
> I wish the team continued success.
>
> Sincerely,
> James Torres

**What it's doing:** When the relationship is strained, shorter is safer. There's
no gratitude paragraph to sound insincere, and no acknowledgment section that
invites scrutiny. This letter does exactly one job — it's a formal record — and
stops. Any grievances belong in an exit interview or a private conversation, not
a document that becomes part of your permanent HR file.

## Example 4: Retiring after a long tenure

> Dear Frank,
>
> After 22 years with Bristow Manufacturing, I am writing to formally announce
> my retirement, effective October 1. I wanted to give the team ample time to
> plan for the transition, which is why I'm providing three months' notice
> rather than the standard two weeks.
>
> These years have shaped my career in ways I couldn't have predicted when I
> started on the floor in 2004. I'm glad to help identify and train my
> replacement in whatever way is most useful over the coming months.
>
> With gratitude,
> Susan Whitfield

**What it's doing:** Longer notice periods are appropriate — and expected — for
senior or long-tenured roles where a replacement search takes months, not weeks.
The gratitude here is proportional to the relationship: two decades earns more
than one sentence, but it still stays specific rather than sentimental.

## Example 5: Immediate resignation (no notice period)

> Dear Ravi,
>
> I am resigning from my position as Support Specialist, effective immediately.
> Due to personal circumstances, I am unable to provide the standard notice
> period.
>
> I apologize for any disruption this causes and am available by phone this
> week if there's information I can hand off remotely.
>
> Regards,
> Ana Kowalski

**What it's doing:** States the immediate departure plainly in the first
sentence rather than burying it. "Personal circumstances" is enough explanation
— you don't owe more detail than that, even in an abrupt exit. Offering limited,
specific availability (a phone call, this week) shows good faith without
obligating you to more than you can give.

## Example 6: Resigning from a remote position

> Dear Tom,
>
> I'm writing to formally resign from my role as Customer Success Manager,
> effective two weeks from today, September 5.
>
> Since we're fully remote, I want to flag the specific handoff items now: my
> current account list, the onboarding docs I maintain, and login access that
> will need to be transferred before my last day. I'll send a detailed handoff
> doc by end of week.
>
> Thanks for the opportunity to build the customer success process here from
> scratch.
>
> Best,
> Devon Park

**What it's doing:** Remote resignations benefit from more explicit logistics
because there's no in-person handoff to fall back on. Naming specific systems
and documents up front prevents a scramble over Slack in the final days.

## What stays constant across all six

Every version above still contains the same core elements: a clear statement of
resignation, a specific last day, and a professional close. The differences are
in tone, length, and how much is offered beyond the minimum — not in the
structure itself.

If you're not sure which tone fits your situation, err toward shorter and more
neutral. A resignation letter is a formal record, not the place to process how
you feel about leaving.
