A resume gets rejected for one of two reasons: the candidate genuinely isn't
qualified, or the bullet points fail to show qualifications that are actually
there. The second case is far more common — and far more fixable — than most
job seekers realize.

Here are the mistakes that do the most damage, in roughly the order they cost
you interviews.

## Mistake 1: Describing duties instead of results

**What it looks like:** "Responsible for handling inbound customer support
tickets across phone and email channels."

**Why it fails:** This describes what your job was, not what you accomplished
in it. A recruiter reading this can't tell if you were good at the job or just
present for it. Two people can hold the same job title and produce wildly
different results — this bullet doesn't let the reader tell which one you were.

**The fix:** "Resolved an average of 45 tickets per day at a 96% first-contact
resolution rate, cutting the team's backlog in half over one quarter." Same
job, but now it's evidence instead of a job description.

## Mistake 2: Quantifying activity instead of impact

**What it looks like:** "Attended 30+ client meetings" or "Sent over 500 cold
emails per quarter" or "Completed 12 training modules."

**Why it fails:** These are real numbers, which makes them easy to mistake for
strong bullets — but they measure effort, not outcome. A reader can't tell if
30 client meetings produced any revenue, or if 500 cold emails got a single
reply. Activity metrics answer "how busy were you," which isn't the question
a hiring manager is actually asking.

**The fix:** Pair the activity with what it produced, or replace it entirely.
"Attended 30+ client meetings" becomes "Closed $340K in renewals through
30+ client meetings this year." If the activity genuinely didn't lead anywhere
measurable, it's usually not worth a bullet on its own.

## Mistake 3: Using jargon your reader won't recognize

**What it looks like:** "Led migration to Atlas 3.0 as part of the Q3 OKR
initiative" or "Drove adoption of the new SFA workflow across the pod."

**Why it fails:** Internal tool names, project codenames, and team-specific
acronyms mean nothing to someone outside your company — including the
recruiter doing a first pass and the hiring manager from a different
department. A bullet that only makes sense to your former coworkers isn't
communicating anything to the person reading it now.

**The fix:** Translate internal terms into what they actually mean to an
outsider. "Led migration to Atlas 3.0" becomes "Led migration to a new
internal billing platform, cutting invoice processing time by 35%." The
specific proper noun almost never matters — the outcome does.

## Mistake 4: Keyword stuffing without context

**What it looks like:** Bullets crammed with every tool and skill from the job
posting, strung together without a real sentence structure: "Utilized Salesforce,
HubSpot, and Excel to manage cross-functional stakeholder relationships and
drive KPI improvements."

**Why it fails:** ATS systems do look for keywords, but this approach reads as
obviously manipulated to any human who gets past the filter — and hiring
managers are the ones who make the actual decision. It also fails to show
whether you used these tools well or just touched them once.

**The fix:** Work keywords into bullets that also describe a real outcome:
"Used Salesforce automation to cut manual data entry by 10 hours per week
across a 12-person sales team." The keyword is there, but it's doing real work
in a real sentence.

## Mistake 5: Claiming sole credit for team work

**What it looks like:** "Launched a $2M product line" when the launch was
actually the work of a 15-person cross-functional team, and the bullet's
author was one contributor among many.

**Why it fails:** This one doesn't just weaken a bullet — it creates risk. If
an interviewer asks a single follow-up question ("walk me through how you
personally did that"), the gap between the claim and the reality becomes
obvious fast, and it damages credibility for every other bullet on the page,
not just that one.

**The fix:** Scope the claim to your actual role without shrinking the
achievement. "Led pricing strategy for a $2M product line launch, coordinating
with engineering and sales on go-to-market timing" is still an impressive
bullet — and it's one you can defend in detail if asked, because it's true.

## Mistake 6: The same structure for every bullet

**What it looks like:** Every single bullet follows the exact pattern "Verb +
task + result," in the same order, with similar length, for an entire page.

**Why it fails:** This isn't disqualifying on its own, but it makes a resume
monotonous to read, and monotony reduces how much a reader actually retains
by the time they reach the bottom of the page.

**The fix:** Vary where the number lands in the sentence, and don't force
every bullet into the identical formula. Some can lead with the result
("Cut onboarding time by 40% by..."), others with the action. Small variation
keeps a reader's attention across a full page of bullets.

## Mistake 7: Irrelevant achievements for the specific role

**What it looks like:** Leading with your strongest bullet regardless of
whether it's relevant to the job you're applying for.

**Why it fails:** Your best bullet for a marketing role and your best bullet
for an operations role are probably different, even if both jobs are on your
resume. A generic "greatest hits" resume sent to every job undersells you for
each specific one.

**The fix:** Reorder and rewrite bullets per application to foreground the
achievements most relevant to that specific posting. This takes more time per
application, but it's the difference between a resume that's technically
accurate and one that's actually persuasive for the role in front of it.

## The through-line

Every mistake above comes down to the same root cause: describing the job
instead of proving you did it well. Fixing that means grounding every bullet
in a specific, ideally quantified outcome — even when the honest number is
modest.
