Generic cold email templates fail for a predictable reason: the person reading
them can tell it's generic. The fix isn't a better template — it's a template
built for the specific reason you're reaching out, with room left to make it
yours.

Here are five, organized by use case, with notes on what each one is doing.

## Template 1: Requesting a meeting with a prospect

> Subject: Quick question about [specific process at their company]
>
> Hi Dana,
>
> I noticed [Company] recently [specific trigger — expanded to a new market,
> hired a VP of Ops, published a case study about scaling support]. Companies
> at that stage usually run into [specific problem your product solves] around
> now.
>
> We helped [similar company] cut [specific metric] by [specific number] in
> about six weeks. I have a two-question read on whether the same approach
> would apply to your setup — worth 15 minutes this week?
>
> Best,
> [Name]

**What it's doing:** Opens with a trigger specific to that company, not a
generic pain point. The proof point uses a real number, not "significant
results." The ask is small and time-boxed — 15 minutes, not "let's connect."

## Template 2: Reconnecting with a dormant contact

> Subject: Been a while — quick update
>
> Hi Marcus,
>
> It's been a couple of years since we worked together on [specific project].
> I saw you moved to [new role/company] — congrats.
>
> I'm reaching out because [specific reason: you're now doing something
> relevant to their new role, you have a mutual connection, you saw them post
> about a problem you can help with]. No agenda beyond that — would be good to
> catch up if you have 20 minutes sometime.
>
> [Name]

**What it's doing:** Names the shared history specifically instead of a vague
"hope you're well." The trigger for reaching out now (not six months ago) is
explicit, which answers the unspoken "why is this person emailing me" question.

## Template 3: Cold outreach for a job or freelance opportunity

> Subject: [Specific skill] for [specific need you noticed]
>
> Hi Sam,
>
> I saw [Company] is [hiring for X / expanding into Y / launching Z] — that
> usually means you need someone who can [specific capability]. I've spent the
> last three years doing exactly that at [Company/context], most recently
> [specific result].
>
> I know you're not necessarily looking for outside help right now, but I'd
> rather ask than assume. Open to a short call to see if there's a fit?
>
> [Name]
> [Portfolio/LinkedIn link]

**What it's doing:** Leads with what the company needs, not what the sender
wants. The acknowledgment ("not necessarily looking") lowers the pressure and
reads as self-aware rather than presumptuous. One specific result stands in for
a full resume.

## Template 4: Pitching a partnership or collaboration

> Subject: Idea for [specific overlap between your audiences]
>
> Hi Priya,
>
> Your [newsletter/product/community] and what we're building at [Company]
> both reach [specific overlapping audience], but from different angles —
> yours on [their angle], ours on [your angle].
>
> I think there's a version of [specific collaboration idea: co-hosted
> webinar, cross-promotion, bundled offer] that would work well for both
> audiences. Happy to sketch out specifics if it's interesting — no pressure
> either way.
>
> [Name]

**What it's doing:** States the overlap in one sentence instead of a paragraph
of context. Proposes a specific collaboration format rather than "let's
partner," which is too vague to act on. "No pressure either way" removes the
obligation to respond enthusiastically.

## Template 5: Cold email to a potential investor or advisor

> Subject: [Company] — [one-line traction metric]
>
> Hi Alex,
>
> We're building [one-sentence description] and just hit [specific traction
> metric: revenue, users, retention number]. [Mutual connection] mentioned you
> invest in [specific space], which is why I'm reaching out directly.
>
> I'd value 20 minutes to get your read on [specific question — not "raise
> money," something narrower like "our go-to-market for enterprise"]. No
> deck required for this one — just want your take.
>
> [Name]

**What it's doing:** The subject line front-loads the one thing that makes this
worth opening — a real number. Naming the mutual connection establishes context
immediately. Asking for a specific opinion rather than a general "chat" gives
the recipient an easy reason to say yes.

## What all five have in common

Every template above:

- Opens with something specific to the recipient, not the sender
- States one small, low-friction ask
- Includes a real number, name, or detail instead of a vague claim
- Is short enough to read in under 20 seconds
- Could not be sent, unedited, to a different recipient

That last point is the real test. If you can copy-paste a cold email template
to ten different people without changing anything beyond the name, it will read
that way to all ten of them.
